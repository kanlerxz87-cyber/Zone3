What you can say about a product you have not used

The FTC's one-line rule on experience claims, Amazon's accuracy clause, and the editorial standard this desk follows instead of fake hands-on reviews.

The short version

  • The FTC's rule is short: you cannot talk about your experience with a product you have not tried.
  • Amazon separately forbids inaccurate, overbroad or misleading claims about any product - regardless of who is speaking.
  • The way through is attribute-based writing: say what the listing and the official pages say, link every claim, and never fake the hands-on part.

The rule that ends fake reviews

The FTC's guidance to influencers and reviewers is unusually plain: "You can't talk about your experience with a product you haven't tried." S07 The same guidance requires financial relationships with a brand to be disclosed S07, and it requires the disclosure to be placed "so it's hard to miss" S07. The Endorsement Guides behind that guidance were revised in 2023 and remain the current standard S24.

This is not a technicality. A review that invents a weekend of testing is a deceptive endorsement, and the FTC's material-connection rule applies to affiliate links exactly as it does to free samples: the commission is the relationship, so the relationship must be disclosed S07.

Amazon's own accuracy clause

Beyond the FTC's rules, Associates operate under Amazon's Program Policies, which add their own floor: "You must not make inaccurate, overbroad, deceptive or otherwise misleading claims about any Product, an Amazon Site, or the existence of a commission on Amazon purchases." S02

Read together, the two rules define a workable standard for a site that has not touched the product: stick to what the record supports, keep the commission relationship disclosed, and let the reader see the difference between a documented attribute and an opinion.

The standard this desk follows

Every article here is written from official pages: the listings themselves, Amazon's policy documents and the FTC's guidance. Attributes - capacity, basket count, named functions - are read from the listing title or the listing page, and linked S14 S15 S16. Rules - what may be displayed, what must be disclosed - are linked to the policy document that states them.

What the desk never writes: 'we tested', 'in our kitchen', 'after three weeks of daily use'. Nobody on this project has used the products. Where the record is silent - performance, durability, real-world cook times - the article says so instead of inventing colour.

The disclosure that Amazon's Operating Agreement requires - 'As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases.' - appears on every page of this site, and the FTC's placement rule (hard to miss, close to the recommendation) is applied to the product pointers on the marketing pages S01 S07.

A checklist for honest product copy

For every sentence about a product, ask three questions. Can I point to the page that states it? Does it claim an experience nobody here had? Would the sentence survive if the reader could see my sources? If any answer is no, the sentence does not ship.

That discipline is also what separates a useful site from the templated filler that searchers have learned to skip: specifics with sources read differently from adjectives without any, and readers can tell.
