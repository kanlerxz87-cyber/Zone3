Meat thermometers / Reading the label

What do “1 Sec” and “±0.5℉” on a listing actually tell you?

Two thermometer listings put figures in the title. Here is the difference between a number you can go and check and one you take on trust.

THE SHORT ANSWER

A figure in a title is a claim about a measurement, not the measurement. Quote the words, then look for what the number was taken against — method, conditions and which part of the tool it describes. Until that is in front of you, it is a brand’s wording about its own listing, not a basis for comparing two brands.

Start with what the titles actually carry.

One listing reads, in part: “1 Sec Professional Digital Meat Thermometer, ±0.5℉ Accuracy | NIST&NSF Certified Food Thermometer … Highly Waterproof”. The other reads, in part: “3 Ultra-Thin Probes | WiFi Unlimited Range | 1000°F Heat Safe | 5 Sensors”. Those strings were read off the listing titles; the exact wording is Amazon’s page to change, and a title is marketing space rather than a specification sheet.

Now look at how little each figure answers on its own. “1 Sec” is a duration with no stated starting point — immersion to first digit shown, or to a stable reading? “±0.5℉” is a tolerance with no stated reference — one calibration point, a range, or a typical kitchen? The title does not say, and the two questions have different answers in a manual.

  • Which words in the title are figures, and which are names or adjectives?
  • Does the listing state what a figure was measured against, or only what it is?
  • Do two listings in one shortlist use the same word for two different things?
  • Where would you look for the method — a manual, a spec table, or nowhere?

A response time, a tolerance and a certification are three different claims.

They are easy to read as a single number for “how good it is”. They are not that. A response time describes how fast a display settles; a tolerance describes how far a reading might sit from a reference; a certification or calibration mention describes a relationship between a device and a standard, which is a separate question from whether that relationship is something you can check on the unit that arrives. Two brands can both be accurate about their own wording and still not be comparable, because the words measure different things.

The same care applies to the connected listing. “Unlimited range” is a word about connectivity, not a distance — unlimited by what: the network, the plan, your home? “1000°F heat safe” names a component limit, not a cooking method, and a probe count in a title tells you what ships in the box, not what the app will show you while dinner cooks.

  • For each figure, name its subject: the display, the probe, the cable, the app, or the box?
  • Is there a condition attached (temperature, distance, battery state), or only a number?
  • Would you accept the same sentence about a competitor’s product without the condition?

What a title cannot do for you.

It cannot become a claim by us. Amazon’s Program Policies prohibit inaccurate, overbroad, deceptive or otherwise misleading statements about a product, and the FTC’s guidance on endorsements is blunt about experience: you cannot describe a product you have not used. So this page quotes titles and stops there — including this sentence.

It also cannot tell you what to cook food to. When a reading is the thing you are relying on, the reference for safe minimum temperatures is published food-safety guidance for the category of food, not a listing. If you want the check itself explained — where a probe goes, and why a timer is not a temperature — that is a separate question with its own page here.

  • Before repeating any figure from a title, open the exact model’s own instructions.
  • If the condition behind a figure is missing, treat the figure as unverified rather than as a weak yes.
  • Compare two products only where both state the same kind of figure in the same way.
