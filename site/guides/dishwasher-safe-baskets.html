Which air fryer parts actually go in the dishwasher — checked line by line

The single most common reason a counter appliance stops getting used is the washing up. "Dishwasher safe" is printed on plenty of listings, but it is usually a claim about one part, not about the machine. Here is what each of these three lines states, word for word, and what none of them states.

The pattern worth noticing

Two of the three lines make the claim about baskets. That is normal, and it is a useful limit to carry into the shop: the housing on every air fryer on the market contains the heating element and the fan, and it is wiped, not washed. The claim that changes your week is narrower than it sounds — it means the parts you touch food in can go on the top rack, according to the listing.

What the claim does not cover is the thing people actually argue about: nonstick plus repeated dishwasher cycles. We are not going to tell you what happens to these coatings after two years, because that is an experience claim and nobody here has used them. What you can do in ninety seconds is open the listing's own care and cleaning instructions and see whether they say hand-wash, dishwasher-safe, or both, and whether they name a rack position. If the listing's care section and the title disagree, trust the care section — it is written for the machine, the title is written for search.

Adjacent claims that are worth checking if they matter to you

  • "PFAS-free" / ceramic coating. None of the three lines above uses the term. Some competing listings in the same Amazon category do — the Cosori TurboBlaze line in the category reads "PFAS-Free Ceramic Coating", and another in the same capture reads "90°-450°F, 5 Fan Speeds". If that is a deal-breaker or a deal-maker for you, search the listing text for the specific words; silence on our three lines is not a claim that the coating is anything in particular. CBA-S06
  • Windows. Only P1 states a viewing feature — "Easy View Windows". If you are the sort of cook who opens the basket constantly, that is a cleaning-adjacent feature: fewer opens means less grease on the front.
  • Count of parts. Two baskets is two things to wash; P1's own line says the baskets are independent. That is a real cost of the dual-basket layout and it belongs in the decision, next to its obvious benefit.

What this page will not claim

That the P2 accessories are or are not dishwasher-safe. The text we read does not say. We left the cell blank rather than inferring it from the machine's category. It is on the open-items list, not in the copy.

That any coating lasts, peels, stains or wears. Longevity claims need either a long test or the manufacturer's own warranty text; this site has neither, and it does not repeat anecdotes from review pages — customer reviews and star ratings may only be displayed when obtained through Amazon's API, which this site has no credentials for. CBA-S09

That one machine is easier to clean overall. "Overall" would need the housing, the racks, the crumb tray and the window in one comparison. We can only compare what the lines state, and they state different things.

The clean-your-shortlist test

  1. Open each listing and search the page for "dishwasher". Note what noun the sentence is about: basket, tray, rack, plate, accessory.
  2. Count the parts that touch food. Two baskets, one basket, or six accessories plus a cavity — those are three different Sunday-evening jobs.
  3. Check whether the listing states a care section that contradicts the title, and believe the care section.
  4. If a part's status is unstated, assume it is hand-wash until the listing says otherwise. That is the conservative reading, and it is the one this page uses.
